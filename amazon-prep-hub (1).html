What the SDE intern loop looks like

For US SDE intern roles, the virtual onsite is typically one or two back to back 45 to 60 minute rounds, each blending behavioral and technical. A few candidates report three rounds. Recent recruiters have also added a short generative AI question at the end.

  • Roughly 10 to 15 minutes of behavioral up front (one to two LP questions, with deep follow ups).
  • 25 to 30 minutes of coding on one Medium problem, sometimes two Easy ones.
  • A few minutes for your questions back to the interviewer.
  • If it is a second round, a different interviewer covers different LPs and a different problem type.

The mental model that wins

Every Amazon answer, technical or behavioral, follows a similar arc. Internalize this and your responses will sound structured without sounding rehearsed.

Step 1
Clarify
Restate the problem, ask about constraints, confirm assumptions.
Step 2
Brute force
State the obvious solution with complexity. Never skip this.
Step 3
Optimize
Name the pattern. Walk the new approach before coding.
Step 4
Code clean
Readable names, no nested mess. Talk while you type.
Step 5
Test
Walk through a normal case, an edge case, an empty input.
Reality check. The single most common failure mode is rushing to code before clarifying. Slow down on Step 1. Interviewers note candidates who probe inputs and constraints before touching the editor.

Your 14 day battle plan

Built for a focused fortnight. Days 1 through 10 build technical and behavioral muscle in parallel. Days 11 through 13 are mock and stress test. Day 14 is light review and rest. Check off each day as you finish it.

Treat the daily hours as a target, not a ceiling. Some days will be heavier (mock rounds, story writing). If you fall behind, prioritize STAR stories and Medium LeetCode practice over volume.

GenAI question prep

Amazon added a GenAI question to many SDE intern loops in 2026. Sometimes a behavioral probe about your usage. Sometimes a small design question. You will not be expected to be an AI researcher, but you should have considered opinions and concrete examples ready.

Likely questions

About your usage

  • How do you use AI tools in your development workflow?
  • Tell me about a time an AI tool helped you solve a problem you were stuck on.
  • When do you choose not to use AI tools, and why?
  • How do you verify the code an AI tool gives you?

About AI in software engineering

  • Where do you see AI changing software engineering in the next 3 to 5 years?
  • What are the limitations of LLMs in code generation?
  • How do you handle hallucinations when using AI tools?
  • What kinds of tasks are AI tools still bad at?

Design and product flavored probes

  • If you were to build a GenAI feature into a product you use today, what would it be and why?
  • How would you evaluate whether an LLM powered feature is working well?
  • What is the difference between prompt engineering and fine tuning in a sentence?
  • How would you reduce LLM costs in a production feature?

Talking points to prepare

Have a real workflow story

Pick a concrete moment when you used Claude, Copilot, or ChatGPT to solve a real problem. Be specific: what you asked, what you got, what you verified, what you rewrote. Generic "I use AI for productivity" answers signal nothing.

Show balanced thinking

Mention both productivity wins (faster scaffolding, better error explanations, learning new libraries) and real limitations (subtle bugs in generated code, plausible looking nonsense, weakness on novel or large codebases, security concerns). Pure cheerleading or pure skepticism both read poorly. Calibrated takes win.

Know one design pattern

Be ready to sketch a basic RAG (retrieval augmented generation) flow at a high level: user query, embed, vector search against a knowledge base, pass top results into LLM context, return answer. Knowing this one pattern covers most "build an AI feature" probes.

Frame around customers

Whatever AI answer you give, tie it back to a customer benefit. Amazon is going to score you on Customer Obsession even in technical questions. "LLMs are useful because they help users get answers faster" beats "LLMs are useful because they are cool tech."

What not to say. Do not claim you can build an LLM from scratch. Do not say you would just paste customer data into a public AI service. Do not pretend you have never used AI tools. Do not overstate. Calibration is the signal.

Day of cheat sheet

Print this. Read it the morning of. It collects everything you should remember after two weeks of prep into one page.

The night before

  • Test your camera, mic, and the coding environment Amazon sent you a link to. Many candidates have lost rounds to broken setups.
  • Print or open your five STAR stories in a doc you can glance at. Do not hide them. Interviewers can see your eyes.
  • Reread the official 16 Leadership Principles. Just the official one liners.
  • Lay out water, a notebook, a pen, and a charged laptop. Pick the room with the best lighting.
  • Sleep. Resist the urge to grind one more problem. You either know it or you don't at this point.

Thirty minutes before

  • Light snack, water nearby, bathroom done.
  • Join the meeting 5 minutes early. Be muted, camera off, ready to flick on at the time.
  • Take 60 seconds for box breathing. Four in, four hold, four out, four hold.
  • Open a blank scratch doc. You will use it to track the interviewer's requirements during the problem.

Behavioral round mantras

  • Speak in I, not we. The action section is yours alone.
  • Spend 20 percent on situation, 60 on action, 20 on result. If you are at three minutes and still on situation, abort.
  • Numbers everywhere you can. Real metrics are believable. Vague claims are not.
  • Pause before answering. Two beats of silence is fine. Better than rambling.
  • Welcome follow up questions. Amazon interviewers dig three or four layers deep. Have a second example ready.

Technical round mantras

  • Restate the problem in your own words before touching code. Confirm.
  • Ask about input size, edge cases, expected behavior on bad input.
  • State a brute force first. Even if it is bad. Then optimize from there.
  • Name the pattern out loud when you spot it. Two pointer, sliding window, BFS, DP, monotonic stack.
  • Talk while you code. Silent typing is the worst signal you can give.
  • Run two test cases manually at the end. Walk through the code as a human compiler.
  • If stuck, narrate your stuckness. Interviewers can hint when they hear specific confusion. They cannot when you are just frozen.

Hard nos

  • Do not use AI assistants during the interview. Detection ends the loop. Amazon has explicitly warned against this in 2026.
  • Do not paraphrase the LPs in your own words mid answer to show off. Just answer the question.
  • Do not invent stories. They follow up four layers deep. They will catch you.
  • Do not skip clarifying questions to look fast. Looking fast and being wrong is the worst combination.
  • Do not finish a coding problem and go silent. Always test, always discuss complexity, always offer a tradeoff.

Smart questions to ask at the end

  • What does a strong intern look like on your team three months in?
  • What is the team currently working on, and what would I likely touch as an intern?
  • How does the team measure success for an intern project?
  • What is one thing about working at Amazon that surprised you?
  • How does the Leadership Principle of (pick one specifically) show up in day to day work on your team?
